### Band 8.5 Model Response (184 words)
The line graph delineates the output of electricity derived from three renewable modalities—hydroelectric, wind, and solar photovoltaic systems—in a European nation between 2010 and 2025, calibrated in Terawatt-hours (TWh).

Overall, total renewable electricity generation witnessed substantial expansion across the fifteen-year period. While hydroelectric generation maintained steady, plateaued dominance during the initial years, wind power experienced exponential acceleration to emerge as the pre-eminent source, closely mirrored by a dramatic late surge in solar output.

In 2010, hydroelectricity represented the predominant contributor at 60 TWh, whereas wind and solar generated modest yields of 25 TWh and 5 TWh respectively. Over the subsequent decade, hydroelectric capacity rose marginally, culminating at 72 TWh by 2025.

Conversely, wind generation exhibited remarkable momentum, overtaking hydroelectricity around 2018 at approximately 68 TWh before ascending steeply to attain a peak of 115 TWh in 2025. Solar power displayed an even steeper trajectory post-2016: after lingering below 20 TWh, it accelerated markedly over the final six years, eclipsing hydroelectricity by 2024 to conclude at 90 TWh.

## Task 2: Higher Education Funding Models (Tuition vs Free Access)

### Task Prompt
*You should spend about 40 minutes on this task.*

Write about the following topic:

**Some people argue that tertiary education should be fully funded by the state for all citizens, while others contend that university students should pay tuition fees because higher education primarily confers financial benefits upon the individual.**

**Discuss both views and give your own opinion.**

Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience.

*Write at least 250 words.*

---

### Band 6.0 Model Response (272 words)
Nowadays, many people debate whether university should be free or if students must pay tuition. This is a very controversial issue in modern society. In this essay, I will discuss both sides of the argument and give my own opinion.

On the one hand, some people believe that governments should pay for all university fees. The main reason is that education is a human right. If universities are completely free, poor students from poor families can study and get good jobs. For example, in some European countries like Germany, universities are free, so young people do not have student debt. Furthermore, having more educated doctors and engineers is good for the economy. Therefore, the state should invest money in education.

On the other hand, other people think students must pay for their own education. They argue that going to university gives high salaries in the future. Because university graduates earn much more money than high school graduates, it is not fair for normal taxpayers who do not go to university to pay for rich students. In addition, when universities charge tuition fees, they have more money to buy modern equipment and computers, which improves teaching quality.

In my opinion, I think a mixed system is the best choice. Completely free education can cause big taxes for citizens, but very high fees stop poor students from studying. Governments should give free scholarships to talented poor students, while rich students should pay reasonable fees. In conclusion, both views have good points, but a balanced policy is best.

#### Analytical Pedagogical Critique of Band 6.0 Response
- **Task Response (TR: Band 6.0)**: Addresses all parts of the prompt with a clear position, but arguments are somewhat superficial. Examples lack specificity (e.g., generic reference to Germany).
- **Coherence & Cohesion (CC: Band 6.0)**: Paragraphing is logical, but cohesive devices are mechanical: repeated formulaic linkers (*On the one hand, On the other hand, Furthermore, In addition, In conclusion*). Lacks sophisticated referential pronouns.
- **Lexical Resource (LR: Band 6.0)**: Basic to intermediate vocabulary with frequent repetitions (*poor students, poor families, free education, big taxes*). Lacks academic collocations.
- **Grammatical Range & Accuracy (GRA: Band 6.0)**: Mix of simple and compound sentences with generally good accuracy, but rare complex subordination. Repetitive modal structures (*should, must*).

### Band 8.5 Model Response (338 words)
The financial architecture of tertiary education remains a subject of intense socio-economic debate. While proponents of universal public subsidization assert that higher education is a non-excludable public good that accelerates meritocratic social mobility, detractors maintain that because degree holders capture substantial private wage premiums, tuition costs should be borne primarily by individual beneficiaries. This essay examines both perspectives before arguing that an income-contingent graduate contribution model offers the most equitable fiscal equilibrium.

Advocates of state-funded higher education anchor their rationale in positive macroeconomic externalities. A highly credentialed workforce fuels domestic innovation, attracts high-value foreign direct investment, and elevates national productivity. When tertiary matriculation is unencumbered by upfront tuition barriers, systemic socioeconomic stratification is alleviated, enabling academically gifted students from marginalized cohorts to enter high-status professions such as medicine, law, and engineering. Jurisdictions such as Norway and Germany demonstrate that public investment in human capital generates compounding returns via robust long-term fiscal revenue and diminished social welfare dependency.

Conversely, proponents of fee-based tertiary systems contend that universal state subsidization constitutes a regressive wealth transfer. Empirical labor-market data consistently confirms that university graduates command significantly higher lifetime earnings than non-graduate peers. Consequently, utilizing general tax revenues—contributed disproportionately by working-class laborers who may never attend university—to underwrite elite individual credentialing is fiscally inequitable. Furthermore, institutions reliant exclusively upon constrained state allocations frequently suffer from infrastructure underinvestment, whereas market-calibrated tuition revenues empower universities to expand research laboratories and recruit world-class faculty.

In my view, the dilemma is falsely framed as an absolute binary between unregulated marketization and blanket state paternalism. The optimal framework is an income-contingent deferred repayment mechanism, exemplified by the Australian HECS system. Under this paradigm, higher education remains free at the point of delivery, eliminating socioeconomic entry barriers, but graduates contribute a progressive percentage of their income once their post-graduation earnings exceed a dignified national threshold. This model reconciles equal societal opportunity with progressive fiscal fairness.
