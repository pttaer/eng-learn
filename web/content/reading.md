# Passage
Today is Saturday. I go to the market with my mother. It is big and busy. There are many people. The market opens at six. We go there early.

I see fruit and vegetables. The apples are red. The bananas are yellow. Apples are two dollars. Bananas are one dollar. My mother buys rice, fish and eggs. I carry the bag. It is a good day. I like the market.

A woman sells flowers. They are pink and white. I want one flower. I ask, "How much is it?" She says, "It is one dollar." I say thank you. We go home at ten.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: A child and the child's mother go to the market. The mother buys food, and the child asks for a flower.
- **Targets**: market, fruit, apples, rice, flower, dollar

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: Who goes to the market?
  A: A child and the child's mother go to the market.
- Q: What colour are the bananas?
  A: The bananas are yellow.
- Q: How much is the flower?
  A: It is one dollar.

## Syntax Dissection
### Sentence 1
- **Sentence**: The apples are red.
- **Subject**: The apples
- **Verb**: are
- **Object**: none
- **Analysis**: The verb "to be" with a colour word. "Apples" is plural, so we use "are". (Danh từ số nhiều đi với "are".)

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: I go to the market with my mother.
- **Subject**: I
- **Verb**: go
- **Object**: none
- **Analysis**: "To the market" shows the place. "With my mother" shows who goes too. (Giới từ "to" chỉ nơi đến, "with" nghĩa là cùng với.)

### Sentence 3
- **Sentence**: How much is it?
- **Subject**: it
- **Verb**: is
- **Object**: none
- **Analysis**: A question about price. "How much" comes first, then "is", then "it". (Câu hỏi giá tiền: "How much" + is + danh từ/it.)

## Sentence Mining
### market
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˈmɑː.kɪt/
- **Definition**: A place where people buy and sell food and other things.
- **Vietnamese**: chợ
- **Context**: I go to the market with my mother.
- **Collocations**: go to the market, a busy market, market day
- **Etymology**: From Latin mercatus (trade).

### vegetables
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˈvedʒ.tə.bəlz/
- **Definition**: Plants that we eat, like carrots and beans.
- **Vietnamese**: rau củ, rau
- **Context**: I see fruit and vegetables.
- **Collocations**: fresh vegetables, eat vegetables, fruit and vegetables
- **Etymology**: From Latin vegetabilis (alive, growing).

### buys
- **Part of Speech**: verb
- **IPA**: /baɪz/
- **Definition**: Gets something and gives money for it.
- **Vietnamese**: mua
- **Context**: My mother buys rice, fish and eggs.
- **Collocations**: buy food, buy a bag, buy and sell
- **Etymology**: From Old English bycgan (to buy).

### flower
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˈflaʊ.ər/
- **Definition**: The coloured part of a plant, often beautiful.
- **Vietnamese**: hoa, bông hoa
- **Context**: I want one flower.
- **Collocations**: a red flower, buy flowers, flower shop
- **Etymology**: From Latin flos (flower).

### dollar
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˈdɒl.ər/
- **Definition**: Money used in the USA and other countries.
- **Vietnamese**: đô la
- **Context**: Bananas are one dollar.

- **Etymology**: From German Taler, a silver coin.

## Synthesis
- **Model Précis**: A child goes to a busy market with the child's mother on Saturday. The mother buys rice, fish and eggs. The child wants a flower for one dollar, and then they go home.
- **Incorporated Vocabulary**: market, vegetables, buy, flower, dollar
- **Syntactic Reconstruction**: I go to the market, and I buy a flower.
- **Copywork Prompt**: Write the question "How much is it?" and the answer "It is one dollar." Change the number.

# Passage
My name is Lan. I get up at six o'clock. I wash my face and eat breakfast. I have bread and an egg. I drink a cup of milk. Then I brush my teeth.

I go to school at seven. I walk with my friend. We study English and maths. I like my teacher. She is kind. At twelve, we eat lunch. After lunch, we play in the yard.

In the afternoon, I go home. I do my homework at the table. My mother cooks dinner at six. We eat together. At ten o'clock, I go to bed. I am very tired.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: Lan has a simple day. She gets up, goes to school, eats with her family and goes to bed.
- **Targets**: breakfast, school, lunch, homework, dinner, bed

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: What time does Lan get up?
  A: She gets up at six o'clock.
- Q: What does Lan have for breakfast?
  A: She has bread, an egg and a cup of milk.
- Q: Who cooks dinner?
  A: Lan's mother cooks dinner.

## Syntax Dissection
### Sentence 1
- **Sentence**: I get up at six o'clock.
- **Subject**: I
- **Verb**: get up
- **Object**: none
- **Analysis**: Simple present tense. "At six o'clock" tells us the time. (Thì hiện tại đơn: "I" + động từ nguyên mẫu; "at" + giờ.)

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: I have bread and an egg.
- **Subject**: I
- **Verb**: have
- **Object**: bread and an egg
- **Analysis**: Simple present tense with "have". "Bread" has no "a" because we cannot count it; "egg" has "an" because it starts with a vowel sound. (Bread là danh từ không đếm được nên không có "a".)

### Sentence 3
- **Sentence**: My mother cooks dinner at six.
- **Subject**: My mother
- **Verb**: cooks
- **Object**: dinner
- **Analysis**: With "my mother" (she), the verb takes -s: cook becomes cooks. (Chủ ngữ ngôi thứ ba số ít thì động từ thêm -s.)

- **Collocations**: eat breakfast, have breakfast, breakfast time
- **Etymology**: From break + fast: to stop not eating during the night.

- **Definition**: School work that a student does at home.
- **Vietnamese**: bài tập về nhà
- **Context**: I do my homework at the table.
- **Collocations**: do homework, finish homework, English homework
- **Etymology**: From home + work.

### dinner

- **IPA**: /ˈdɪn.ər/
- **Definition**: The main meal of the day, eaten in the evening.
- **Vietnamese**: bữa tối
- **Context**: My mother cooks dinner at six.
- **Collocations**: cook dinner, have dinner, dinner time
- **Etymology**: From Old French disner (to eat the first meal).

### teacher

- **IPA**: /ˈtiː.tʃər/
- **Definition**: A person who helps students learn at school.
- **Vietnamese**: giáo viên, cô giáo, thầy giáo
- **Context**: I like my teacher.
- **Collocations**: English teacher, a kind teacher, ask the teacher
- **Etymology**: From teach + -er (a person who does something).

### tired

# Passage
I have a small family. There are six people. My father is a driver. My mother is a nurse. I have one brother and one sister. We live in a small house.

My brother is ten. He likes football. My sister is five. She likes cats. My grandmother lives with us. She is old and happy. Her name is Hoa. She makes tea every morning.

We have dinner together every evening. My mother makes soup. My father tells funny stories. We all laugh. After dinner, we watch TV. I love my family. I am happy at home. We are a happy family.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: This is a small family of six people. They live together and they are happy.
- **Targets**: family, father, mother, brother, sister, grandmother

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: How many people are in the family?
  A: There are six people.
- Q: What is the father's job?
  A: He is a driver.
- Q: What does the grandmother do every morning?
  A: She makes tea.

- **Sentence**: There are six people.
- **Subject**: six people
- **Verb**: are
- **Object**: none

- **Sentence**: My father is a driver.
- **Subject**: My father
- **Verb**: is
- **Object**: a driver
- **Analysis**: The verb "to be" tells us about a person. We use "a" before a job. (Dùng "a" trước tên nghề nghiệp.)

### Sentence 3
- **Sentence**: She likes cats.
- **Subject**: She
- **Verb**: likes
- **Object**: cats
- **Analysis**: Simple present tense. "She" takes -s: like becomes likes. (Với "she" động từ thêm -s.)

# Passage
Good habits make life better. You should drink a lot of water every day. It is also important to eat fruit and vegetables. Many people eat too much sugar, and it is bad for your health. Try to cook at home because it is cheaper and healthier. Fast food is quick, but it is not always good for you.

Exercise is important too, but you do not need an expensive gym. You can walk to work or ride a bike. Last year, I started to run twice a week. At first it was difficult, and I often felt tired. Now I feel stronger and much happier. My friends say that I look better too.

Sleep is also very important. Most adults need about eight hours every night. You should not use your phone just before bed. If you are sick, you must rest and see a doctor. Do not worry about big changes. Small changes can help you live a long and healthy life. Start today, and you will feel the difference soon.

- **Thesis**: Simple daily habits such as drinking water, eating well, exercising and sleeping enough can make you healthier and happier.
- **Targets**: a lot of water, too much sugar, twice a week, at first, see a doctor, healthy life

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: What should you drink every day?

- **Verb**: should drink

- **Verb**: eat
- **Object**: too much sugar
- **Subordinate Clauses**:

### Sentence 3
- **Sentence**: Try to cook at home because it is cheaper and healthier.
- **Subject**: (you, understood)
- **Verb**: Try to cook
- **Object**: (none)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Reason Clause]: because it is cheaper and healthier | Gives two reasons using comparatives.
- **Analysis**: An imperative sentence (advice) with a "because" clause. Ghi chú: "cheaper", "healthier" là so sánh hơn.

- **Subject**: you
- **Verb**: must rest and see
- **Object**: a doctor
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Condition Clause]: If you are sick | Sets the condition for the advice.
- **Analysis**: "If" + present simple, then "must" + verb. Ghi chú: "must" diễn tả điều bắt buộc hoặc rất cần thiết.

# Passage
Tom arrived in a new city last Monday. He wanted to find the museum, but he had no map. His phone was dead, so he could not use it. He asked a woman near the station for help. She was kind and friendly.

"Go straight, then turn left at the bank," she said. "The museum is next to the park." Tom thanked her and walked for ten minutes. But he turned right by mistake. He walked for a long time. Soon he was in a busy street with many shops and noisy cars. He did not know where he was.

He felt lost and a little worried. He sat down for a moment. Then he saw a policeman and asked again. The policeman smiled and showed him the way on a map. "It is behind you," he said. At last, Tom found the museum. The door was locked and a sign said closed. It was closed, so he had a coffee and went back to the hotel.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: A visitor with no map and a dead phone gets lost after a wrong turn, but a helpful policeman shows him the way.
- **Targets**: go straight, turn left, next to, by mistake, a little worried, at last

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: Why could Tom not use his phone?
  A: It was dead.
- Q: What mistake did Tom make?
  A: He turned right instead of left.
- Q: Was the museum open when Tom found it?
  A: No, it was closed.

## Syntax Dissection
### Sentence 1
- **Sentence**: He wanted to find the museum, but he had no map.
- **Subject**: He
- **Verb**: wanted to find
- **Object**: the museum
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Contrast Clause with "but"]: but he had no map | Shows the problem.
- **Analysis**: Two past simple clauses joined by "but". Ghi chú: "want to" + động từ nguyên mẫu = muốn làm gì.

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: His phone was dead, so he could not use it.
- **Subject**: His phone
- **Verb**: was
- **Object**: (none; "dead" is a complement)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Result Clause with "so"]: so he could not use it | Shows the result of the dead phone.
- **Analysis**: Cause then result with "so". Ghi chú: "could not" là phủ định quá khứ của "can"; "dead" ở đây nghĩa là hết pin.

### Sentence 3
- **Sentence**: "Go straight, then turn left at the bank," she said.
- **Subject**: she
- **Verb**: said
- **Object**: "Go straight, then turn left at the bank"
- **Analysis**: The woman speaks in imperatives to give directions. Ghi chú: "Go straight" = đi thẳng, "turn left" = rẽ trái.

### Sentence 4
- **Sentence**: It was closed, so he had a coffee and went back to the hotel.
- **Subject**: It
- **Verb**: was
- **Object**: (none; "closed" is a complement)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Result Clause with "so"]: so he had a coffee and went back to the hotel | Tom's choice after finding the museum closed.
- **Analysis**: One cause ("It was closed") and two actions in a row ("had" and "went"). Ghi chú: "had" và "went" là quá khứ bất quy tắc của "have" và "go".

# Passage
Last weekend, my friend Anna and I went to the seaside. We left home early on Saturday morning because we wanted to avoid the traffic. The train was cheap, so we bought two tickets online. When we arrived, the weather was sunny and warm. We walked along the beach and took many photos. The sea looked blue and beautiful.

At lunch, we ate fish and chips in a small cafe near the harbour. Later, we swam in the sea, but the water was very cold. In the afternoon, it started to rain, so we visited a museum. It was interesting, and the tickets were free. We learned about old boats and the people who worked on them.

In the evening, we were tired but happy. We sat on a bench and watched the sun go down. The train home was late, but we did not mind. Next month, we are going to visit the mountains. I think it will be another great trip.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: Two friends have a cheap, enjoyable weekend at the seaside, and the good experience makes them plan another trip.

### Comprehension Checks

# Passage
Few buildings are designed with solitude in mind, and fewer still with the dignity of being alone. Architects have long prized the open vista, the shared atrium and the glass partition, all of which signal transparency and civic generosity. Yet a structure that exposes its occupants at every turn quietly withholds something people need in order to think: a place where nobody is watching.

Consider the humble threshold. A door that closes, a corridor that bends, a window seat set back from the main room: such details do not merely divide space but grade it, creating a gradient from the communal to the intimate. Sociologists describe this gradient as the foundation of private life, because it lets a person decide, moment by moment, how much of themselves to disclose. Remove the gradient, and every hour becomes a performance.

Historical precedent supports the point. The medieval monastic cell, the scholar's study in a Renaissance townhouse, and the writer's garden shed were all modest in scale yet generous in effect. Their occupants rarely sought isolation for its own sake; they sought a setting in which sustained reflection would not be punctured by obligations. Smallness, paradoxically, was the source of their expansiveness, since a contained room narrows the world to the dimensions of a single problem.

Contemporary designers face a harder brief, for they must accommodate both collaboration and retreat within budgets that reward density. Acoustic booths allow short periods of concentrated work, while reservable rooms signal that withdrawal is legitimate rather than antisocial. What unites these solutions is their acknowledgment that privacy is not an absence of community but a condition that makes meaningful community sustainable.

# Passage
Attention is commonly imagined as a spotlight, yet the metaphor flatters the mind by implying a steady beam under voluntary control. Experimental evidence portrays something more precarious: a limited pool of processing capacity that must be rationed among competing demands, and that depletes quickly when those demands multiply.

Working memory, the mental workspace in which information is held and manipulated, is the most conspicuous bottleneck. Estimates of its capacity have been revised downward over the decades, from the celebrated figure of seven items to perhaps four discrete chunks, and the revision matters because virtually every complex act, whether parsing a contract or following a proof, depends upon keeping several elements simultaneously active.

Cognitive load theory partitions the burden into three components. Intrinsic load reflects the inherent difficulty of the material; extraneous load arises from poor presentation, such as a cluttered slide or a convoluted set of instructions; germane load denotes the effort devoted to building durable schemas. Instructional design succeeds, on this view, when it trims the extraneous component so that scarce capacity may be redirected towards the germane one.

Expertise alters the arithmetic dramatically. A chess master does not scan pieces individually but perceives configurations as unified patterns, thereby compressing dozens of items into a handful of chunks. Novices, lacking such stored patterns, exhaust their capacity almost instantly, which explains why identical material can seem trivial to one person and impenetrable to another. Difficulty, in other words, resides in the relationship between task and mind rather than in the task alone.

Multitasking, so often celebrated as a modern virtue, fares poorly under scrutiny. What feels like parallel processing is in fact rapid alternation, and every switch exacts a toll in time and accuracy as the brain reconfigures its goals and suppresses the residue of the previous task. Habitual multitaskers, curiously, tend to overestimate their own efficiency, a miscalibration that perpetuates the very behaviour impairing them.

Scarcity also shapes judgement outside the laboratory. Researchers studying people under chronic financial strain have found that worry consumes a measurable share of mental capacity, leaving fewer resources for planning and self-restraint. The resulting lapses are frequently mistaken for character flaws, when they more plausibly reflect an overdrawn cognitive budget. Such findings invite humility toward those whose decisions appear shortsighted.

The practical corollaries are neither exotic nor expensive. Reduce the number of simultaneous demands, externalize what can be written down, rehearse skills until they become automatic, and protect intervals of undivided focus. Each measure conserves bandwidth, and bandwidth, once squandered, is recovered only through rest and deliberate design.

In its popular rendering, the ideal demands that officials apply fixed, publicly promulgated rules mechanically, leaving no room for personal judgment. On this view, discretion is not a supplement to legality but its antithesis: wherever an official may choose, the argument runs, arbitrary power lurks.

There is undeniable force in this position. Rules render state power predictable, allowing citizens to plan their lives in the knowledge of how coercion will be deployed. Where discretion is broad, similar cases may be decided differently according to the temperament of whoever happens to preside, and such inconsistency corrodes public confidence in the impartiality of courts. Nevertheless, the conception of law as a closed system of self-applying rules collapses under scrutiny. Rules are drafted in general language by legislators who cannot foresee every contingency, and general language is irreducibly open-textured. In a celebrated example from twentieth-century jurisprudence, a rule forbidding vehicles in a park says nothing conclusive about bicycles, ambulances, or a decommissioned military truck mounted as a war memorial. To deny this is not to eliminate discretion but merely to drive it underground, where it operates unacknowledged and therefore unaccountable.

It is here that the doctrine of precedent reveals its quiet sophistication. Admittedly, precedent can ossify error, binding later courts to reasoning that was flawed at its inception, and critics are right to deplore the inertia this can produce. But the obligation to follow prior decisions, or to distinguish them with articulated reasons, does not abolish discretion; it disciplines it. A court that wishes to depart from an earlier ruling must explain why the earlier reasoning no longer persuades, and that explanation is itself exposed to criticism, appeal, and scholarly commentary. Each judgment becomes a public commitment against which future judgments can be measured, so that discretion, rather than vanishing, is gradually converted into a body of reasoned and contestable norms.

The rule of law, then, is better understood not as the triumph of rules over judgment but as the insistence that judgment be exercised transparently, consistently, and in a form that others can scrutinise. Discretion that must justify itself is not the enemy of legality; it is one of its most demanding expressions.

# Passage
Few of us question the instinct that a person who is not busy is a person who is wasting something. We absorb it early, from teachers who reward diligence and relatives who ask what we have achieved this week, and by adulthood it operates less like an opinion than like a reflex. Yet reflexes are not arguments, and this one deserves to be examined before it is allowed to organize another generation's lives.

Labour is usually praised as though it were a single virtue, when in practice it covers activities of very different worth. Some work feeds, houses and heals people. Much of the rest exists mainly to keep other work in motion: reports that nobody reads, meetings convened to prepare for other meetings, and supervision of tasks that would be completed perfectly well without it.

Historically, the glorification of effort has been convenient for those who benefit from it. A society in which people feel uneasy whenever they rest is a society in which employers need offer fewer inducements, since the workers will police themselves. Guilt is a remarkably cheap form of management. It requires no supervisor, it travels home with the employee each evening, and it persuades even the exhausted that exhaustion is evidence of character.

Technology has weakened the practical case for such devotion. A modern workforce can produce, in a fraction of the hours its grandparents required, more than enough to meet everyone's basic needs. Instead of sharing the gain by shortening the working week, we have tended to raise our expectations of consumption and to keep the old hours, so that productivity has been converted into output rather than into freedom.

Sceptics reply that people given free time would squander it, and the objection contains a grain of truth. Leisure is a skill, and like any skill it atrophies without practice. Someone who has spent decades responding to deadlines may find an empty afternoon oppressive, and may hurry back to the office simply to escape the silence. But this shows that our education has neglected an important capacity, not that the capacity is unimportant.

What does a well-used free hour produce? It may yield a friendship deepened over a long meal, an amateur's competence at the cello, a garden, an argument followed to its conclusion, or simply the attention to notice what one's own life contains. These goods are easy to mock precisely because they resist measurement, yet most people, asked what they would save from a burning house, choose them over their payslips.

A society that wished to be civilized rather than merely busy would therefore treat rest as an achievement to be cultivated, and would judge its prosperity by the quality of the hours people keep for themselves.
