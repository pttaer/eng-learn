# Passage
Few ideals command such reflexive assent as the rule of law, yet few are so frequently misunderstood. In its popular rendering, the ideal demands that officials apply fixed, publicly promulgated rules mechanically, leaving no room for personal judgment. On this view, discretion is not a supplement to legality but its antithesis: wherever an official may choose, the argument runs, arbitrary power lurks.

There is undeniable force in this position. Rules render state power predictable, allowing citizens to plan their lives in the knowledge of how coercion will be deployed. They also constrain the partiality of officials, who might otherwise indulge prejudice under the guise of wisdom. Where discretion is broad, similar cases may be decided differently according to the temperament of whoever happens to preside, and such inconsistency corrodes public confidence in the impartiality of courts. Legal theorists since A. V. Dicey have treated the submission of government to general rules as a bulwark against tyranny, and the historical record offers ample grounds for that wariness.

Nevertheless, the conception of law as a closed system of self-applying rules collapses under scrutiny. Rules are drafted in general language by legislators who cannot foresee every contingency, and general language is irreducibly open-textured. In a celebrated example from twentieth-century jurisprudence, a rule forbidding vehicles in a park says nothing conclusive about bicycles, ambulances, or a decommissioned military truck mounted as a war memorial. Faced with such penumbral cases, a judge must exercise judgment whether she admits it or not. To deny this is not to eliminate discretion but merely to drive it underground, where it operates unacknowledged and therefore unaccountable.

It is here that the doctrine of precedent reveals its quiet sophistication. Admittedly, precedent can ossify error, binding later courts to reasoning that was flawed at its inception, and critics are right to deplore the inertia this can produce. But the obligation to follow prior decisions, or to distinguish them with articulated reasons, does not abolish discretion; it disciplines it. A court that wishes to depart from an earlier ruling must explain why the earlier reasoning no longer persuades, and that explanation is itself exposed to criticism, appeal, and scholarly commentary. Each judgment becomes a public commitment against which future judgments can be measured, so that discretion, rather than vanishing, is gradually converted into a body of reasoned and contestable norms.

The rule of law, then, is better understood not as the triumph of rules over judgment but as the insistence that judgment be exercised transparently, consistently, and in a form that others can scrutinise. Discretion that must justify itself is not the enemy of legality; it is one of its most demanding expressions.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: Although rules make state power predictable and restrain partiality, no system of rules can apply itself; discretion is therefore unavoidable, and the doctrine of precedent preserves the rule of law not by eliminating judgment but by forcing it to be reasoned, public, and consistent.
- **Targets**: reflexive assent, publicly promulgated, antithesis, partiality, bulwark against tyranny, open-textured, penumbral cases, ossify error, contestable norms
### Comprehension Checks
- Q: What is the "popular rendering" of the rule of law that the author challenges?
  A: The view that officials must apply fixed, published rules mechanically, so that any room for official choice counts as arbitrary power.
- Q: Which merits of strict rules does the author concede in paragraph 2?
  A: Rules make state coercion predictable so citizens can plan their lives, and they restrain officials from acting on prejudice disguised as wisdom.
- Q: Why does the author argue that discretion cannot be eliminated?
  A: Because rules are written in general, open-textured language that cannot anticipate every case, so judges facing borderline cases must exercise judgment; denying this only hides discretion and makes it unaccountable.
- Q: How does precedent, in the author's view, reconcile discretion with the rule of law?
  A: By requiring courts to follow or explicitly distinguish earlier decisions, it turns each judgment into a public commitment, converting discretion into a body of reasoned, contestable norms.

## Synthesis
- **Model Précis**: Rules publicly promulgated in advance are a genuine bulwark against arbitrary power, but general language cannot settle penumbral cases, so judicial discretion is inescapable. Precedent may ossify error, yet by obliging courts to follow or distinguish earlier rulings it converts discretion into reasoned, accountable norms, which is what the rule of law truly requires.
- **Incorporated Vocabulary**: promulgate, bulwark, penumbral, ossify, open-textured
- **Syntactic Reconstruction**: While precedent may well ossify the occasional error, to abandon it would not be to banish discretion but merely to relocate it where no one could hold it to account.
