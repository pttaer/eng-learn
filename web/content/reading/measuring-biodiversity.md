# Passage
At first glance, measuring biodiversity might appear to be a simple matter of counting species. In practice, the task is considerably more fraught. Many organisms are small, cryptic, or only seasonally active, and a substantial proportion of the planet's species, particularly among insects, fungi, and microorganisms, are thought to remain undescribed. Any tally of species in a given habitat is therefore best regarded not as a census but as an estimate, and one whose reliability depends heavily on how the sampling was conducted.

Ecologists have long recognised that the number of species recorded tends to rise with sampling effort. A plot surveyed for a single afternoon will almost inevitably yield fewer species than the same plot surveyed across several seasons, which means that raw counts from different studies may not be directly comparable. To mitigate this, researchers frequently construct species accumulation curves, plotting cumulative species against effort; where such a curve appears to level off, it is reasonable to infer that most common species have been detected, although rare taxa may still be missing. Statistical estimators, such as those that extrapolate from the number of species observed only once or twice, can provide a plausible lower bound on true richness, but they rest on assumptions that may not hold in every system. Different estimators applied to the same data may also produce noticeably different figures.

Richness is, moreover, only one dimension of diversity. Two communities containing identical numbers of species may differ markedly if, in one, a single species is overwhelmingly dominant. Indices that incorporate evenness, such as the Shannon and Simpson measures, attempt to capture this distinction, although their values are sensitive to methodological choices and can be difficult to interpret in isolation. Some ecologists have therefore advocated reporting a family of related measures, often expressed as effective numbers of species, rather than privileging any single index. Each choice reflects a judgment about which aspects of diversity matter most.

Newer techniques appear to offer partial remedies, at least for certain groups of organisms. Environmental DNA sampling, in which genetic fragments shed by organisms are recovered from water or soil, can detect elusive species that conventional surveys might overlook. Yet such methods introduce uncertainties of their own: DNA may persist or drift after an organism has departed, and reference databases remain incomplete, so that a detection is perhaps more cautiously interpreted as consistent with a species' presence than as conclusive proof of it. The prevailing view, insofar as one can be discerned, is that no single method suffices, and that estimates of biodiversity are most credible when several imperfect approaches converge.

  A: Because many species are hidden, seasonal or still undescribed, and the number recorded depends heavily on how much and how well the habitat was sampled.
- Q: What does a levelling species accumulation curve allow researchers to infer, and what caveat applies?
  A: That most common species have probably been detected, although rare taxa may still be missing.
- Q: Why is species richness alone an incomplete measure of diversity?
  A: Because two communities with the same number of species can differ greatly in evenness, for example if one species overwhelmingly dominates one community.
- Q: Why is an environmental DNA detection described only as "consistent with" a species' presence?
  A: Because DNA can persist or drift after the organism has left and reference databases are incomplete, so a detection is not conclusive proof.

- **Verb**: is best regarded

- **Analysis**: The passive "is best regarded" is a characteristic hedge, recommending an interpretation rather than asserting a fact. The appended "and one whose..." uses the pro-form "one" to re-open the noun "estimate" and add a qualification, a typical academic move.
