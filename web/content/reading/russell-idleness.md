# Passage
Few of us question the instinct that a person who is not busy is a person who is wasting something. We absorb it early, from teachers who reward diligence and relatives who ask what we have achieved this week, and by adulthood it operates less like an opinion than like a reflex. Yet reflexes are not arguments, and this one deserves to be examined before it is allowed to organize another generation's lives.

Labour is usually praised as though it were a single virtue, when in practice it covers activities of very different worth. Some work feeds, houses and heals people. Much of the rest exists mainly to keep other work in motion: reports that nobody reads, meetings convened to prepare for other meetings, and supervision of tasks that would be completed perfectly well without it.

Historically, the glorification of effort has been convenient for those who benefit from it. A society in which people feel uneasy whenever they rest is a society in which employers need offer fewer inducements, since the workers will police themselves. Guilt is a remarkably cheap form of management. It requires no supervisor, it travels home with the employee each evening, and it persuades even the exhausted that exhaustion is evidence of character.

Technology has weakened the practical case for such devotion. A modern workforce can produce, in a fraction of the hours its grandparents required, more than enough to meet everyone's basic needs. Instead of sharing the gain by shortening the working week, we have tended to raise our expectations of consumption and to keep the old hours, so that productivity has been converted into output rather than into freedom.

Sceptics reply that people given free time would squander it, and the objection contains a grain of truth. Leisure is a skill, and like any skill it atrophies without practice. Someone who has spent decades responding to deadlines may find an empty afternoon oppressive, and may hurry back to the office simply to escape the silence. But this shows that our education has neglected an important capacity, not that the capacity is unimportant.

What does a well-used free hour produce? It may yield a friendship deepened over a long meal, an amateur's competence at the cello, a garden, an argument followed to its conclusion, or simply the attention to notice what one's own life contains. These goods are easy to mock precisely because they resist measurement, yet most people, asked what they would save from a burning house, choose them over their payslips.

A society that wished to be civilized rather than merely busy would therefore treat rest as an achievement to be cultivated, and would judge its prosperity by the quality of the hours people keep for themselves.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: The habit of equating busyness with virtue serves employers and obscures what rising productivity could offer, so a civilized society should cultivate leisure as a skill instead of treating rest as waste.
- **Targets**: diligence, inducements, atrophies, squander, oppressive, productivity, cultivated

### Comprehension Checks

- Q: What does the author conclude from the fact that some people find free time oppressive?

## Syntax Dissection
### Sentence 1
- **Sentence**: Instead of sharing the gain by shortening the working week, we have tended to raise our expectations of consumption and to keep the old hours, so that productivity has been converted into output rather than into freedom.
- **Subject**: we
- **Verb**: have tended
- **Object**: to raise our expectations of consumption and to keep the old hours (coordinated infinitive complements)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Fronted Prepositional Phrase with Gerund]: Instead of sharing the gain by shortening the working week | Names the alternative that was not chosen, nesting a second gerund ("shortening") inside the first.
  - [Adverbial Clause of Result ("so that")]: so that productivity has been converted into output rather than into freedom | Presents the outcome of the two choices as a passive present-perfect clause.
- **Analysis**: A long periodic sentence whose fronted "instead of" phrase sets up the rejected option before the main clause reveals what was done. The closing contrast "into output rather than into freedom" balances two parallel prepositional phrases and ends on the key abstract noun.

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: But this shows that our education has neglected an important capacity, not that the capacity is unimportant.
- **Subject**: this
- **Verb**: shows
- **Object**: that our education has neglected an important capacity
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Noun Clause as Object]: that our education has neglected an important capacity | States the conclusion the author accepts.
  - [Contrastive Noun Clause with "not"]: not that the capacity is unimportant | Rejects the sceptic's alternative reading of the same evidence.
- **Analysis**: Two parallel "that" clauses are set against each other with "not", so the sentence corrects a misreading in a single line. The present-perfect "has neglected" locates the fault in past educational practice.

- **Collocations**: offer an inducement, financial inducement, strong inducement

- **Collocations**: muscles atrophy, skills atrophy, atrophy through disuse

### squander

## Synthesis
- **Model Précis**: The author argues that admiration for constant busyness is an inherited reflex that benefits employers, not a sound principle. Because modern productivity could shorten working hours, the real problem is that people were never taught to use free time well, so leisure should be cultivated as a skill and treated as a mark of a civilized society.
- **Incorporated Vocabulary**: diligence, inducement, atrophy, squander
- **Syntactic Reconstruction**: Although a workforce that prizes diligence above all else needs few inducements to stay busy, the skill of leisure atrophies when it is never practised, and a society that cannot rest well will squander the very productivity it has gained.
