# Passage
Like most of my generation, I was brought up on the saying: "Satan finds some mischief for idle hands to do." Being a highly virtuous child, I believed all that I was told, and acquired a conscience which has kept me working hard down to the present moment. But although my conscience has controlled my actions, my opinions have undergone a revolution. I think that there is far too much work done in the world, that immense harm is caused by the belief that work is virtuous, and that what needs to be preached in modern industrial countries is quite different from what always has been preached.

First of all: what is work? Work is of two kinds: first, altering the position of matter at or near the earth's surface relatively to other such matter; second, telling other people to do so. The first kind is unpleasant and ill-paid; the second is pleasant and highly paid. The second kind is capable of indefinite extension: there are not only those who give orders, but those who give advice as to what orders should be given. In modern industrial society, the morality of work is the morality of slaves, designed by ruling oligarchies to convince the working majority that their toil is a noble moral duty rather than an extractive necessity.

Modern methods of production have given us the possibility of ease and security for all; we have chosen, instead, to have overwork for some and starvation for others. Hitherto we have continued to be as energetic as we were before there were machines; in this we have been foolish, but there is no reason to go on being foolish for ever. The wise use of leisure, it must be conceded, is a product of civilization and education. A person who has worked long hours all his life will become bored if he becomes suddenly idle. But without a considerable amount of leisure a human being is cut off from many of the best things, and civilization itself degenerates into sterile utilitarianism.

## Cold Read
- **Thesis**: The moral veneration of relentless work is an ideological tool historically fabricated by privileged classes to enforce compliance, whereas modern industrial productivity makes a universal four-hour workday and cultivated leisure the true prerequisites for a flourishing civilization.
- **Targets**: sterile utilitarianism, moral veneration, extractive necessity, ruling oligarchies, indefinite extension, wise use of leisure, ideological tool

### Comprehension Checks
- Q: How does Russell anatomize the two fundamental categories of work?
  A: First, physical labor that alters the position of matter on the earth's surface; second, managerial labor that commands or advises others on how to do so.
- Q: According to Russell, what is the historical origin of the belief that work is virtuous?
  A: It was an ideological doctrine promoted by the wealthy and powerful to induce the laboring classes to work willingly beyond subsistence needs.
- Q: Why does Russell argue that modern machine production justifies shortening work hours?
  A: Because mechanized efficiency allows society to fulfill its material needs with minimal labor, liberating time for cultural, intellectual, and creative development.

## Syntax Dissection
### Sentence 1
- **Sentence**: Modern methods of production have given us the possibility of ease and security for all; we have chosen, instead, to have overwork for some and starvation for others.
- **Subject**: Modern methods of production
- **Verb**: have given
- **Object**: us the possibility of ease and security for all
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Coordinated Antithetical Independent Clause (Semicolon)]: we have chosen, instead, to have overwork for some and starvation for others | Introduces the societal paradox through antithetical parallelism ("ease and security for all" vs "overwork for some and starvation for others").
- **Analysis**: Balanced compound sentence linked by semicolon with parenthetical disjunctive adverb ("instead"). Contrasts technological potential with political failure through chiasmus and parallel prepositional phrases ("for all" versus "for some ... for others").

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: But without a considerable amount of leisure a human being is cut off from many of the best things, and civilization itself degenerates into sterile utilitarianism.
- **Subject**: a human being
- **Verb**: is cut off
- **Object**: from many of the best things
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Fronted Prepositional Conditional Modifier]: without a considerable amount of leisure | Sets the essential negative condition.
  - [Coordinated Resultative Clause]: and civilization itself degenerates into sterile utilitarianism | Scales the consequence from individual deprivation to macro-civilizational decay.
- **Analysis**: Compounded predicate where the intensive reflexive pronoun ("itself") emphasizes the collective tragedy, culminating in the evocative conceptual nominalization "sterile utilitarianism".

## Sentence Mining
### utilitarianism
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /juːˌtɪl.ɪˈteə.ri.ə.nɪ.zəm/
- **Definition**: The doctrine that actions are right if they are useful or for the benefit of a majority; practical usefulness divorced from beauty, joy, or spiritual depth.
- **Vietnamese**: chủ nghĩa công lợi, quan điểm thực dụng vị lợi ích trước mắt
- **Context**: Civilization itself degenerates into sterile utilitarianism without leisure.
- **Collocations**: sterile utilitarianism, crude utilitarianism, rigid utilitarianism
- **Etymology**: Latin utilis (useful) + -arian + -ism.

### veneration
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˌven.əˈreɪ.ʃən/
- **Definition**: Great respect, reverence, or profound awe accorded to someone or something considered holy or intrinsically noble.
- **Vietnamese**: sự sùng kính, lòng tôn kính thiêng liêng, sự tôn sùng mù quáng
- **Context**: Immense harm is caused by the moral veneration of ceaseless toil.
- **Collocations**: moral veneration, deep veneration, object of veneration
- **Etymology**: Latin veneratio, from venerari (to worship, revere).

### extractive
- **Part of Speech**: adjective
- **IPA**: /ɪkˈstræk.tɪv/
- **Definition**: Tending toward or characterized by the extraction of resources, value, or labor without reciprocal contribution or replenishment.
- **Vietnamese**: mang tính bòn rút, bóc lột, chỉ khai thác mà không bồi hoàn
- **Context**: Convince the working majority that their toil is a noble duty rather than an extractive necessity.
- **Collocations**: extractive necessity, extractive economy, extractive institutions
- **Etymology**: Latin extractivus, from extrahere (draw out).

### oligarchy
- **Part of Speech**: noun
- **IPA**: /ˈɒl.ɪ.ɡɑː.ki/
- **Definition**: A small group of people having control of a country, organization, or institution, typically governing in their own interest.
- **Vietnamese**: tập đoàn đầu sỏ, chế độ quả đầu, nhóm thiểu số thống trị
- **Context**: Morality designed by ruling oligarchies to protect their accumulated privileges.
- **Collocations**: ruling oligarchies, financial oligarchy, entrench an oligarchy
- **Etymology**: Greek oligarkhia, from oligos (few) + arkhein (to rule).

## Synthesis
- **Model Précis**: Bertrand Russell deconstructs the traditional work ethic as an antiquated dogma used by privileged elites to perpetuate economic exploitation. In the machine age, where technological productivity can readily ensure universal security, liberating human beings from ceaseless toil to pursue cultivated leisure is the essential foundation of genuine culture, creativity, and humane civilization.
- **Incorporated Vocabulary**: utilitarianism, veneration, extractive, oligarchy
- **Syntactic Reconstruction**: While the gospel of relentless toil was systematically promulgated to legitimize extractive economic arrangements, modern industrial surplus renders such self-abnegation an obstacle to civilizational flourishing.
