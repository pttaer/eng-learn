# Passage
Few assets are as prized and as carelessly squandered as sustained attention. Modern organisations pay handsomely for people who can reason through tangled problems, yet they furnish those same people with workplaces engineered to fragment every hour into scraps, each one claimed by a message, a meeting, or a banner sliding across the screen.

The mismatch rests on a misreading of the mind. We tend to picture thought as a broad river that can carry several cargoes at once, whereas careful analysis behaves more like a narrow bridge, admitting one load at a time. What passes for multitasking is, in practice, rapid alternation, and every crossing exacts a toll that the worker rarely notices and never itemises.

That toll is best understood as residue. When a person abandons a half-finished argument to answer a query, part of the mind lingers with the abandoned problem even as the new one demands full allegiance. The result is a divided effort: replies grow terse, inferences are drawn with less scrutiny, and subtle errors slip through unchallenged. Recovering the earlier depth can take far longer than the interruption itself.

Many people respond to this predicament with resolve, promising themselves to ignore the phone and resist the inbox. Yet resolve is a perishable resource, and a strategy that demands its constant renewal is destined to fail by mid-afternoon. A more durable remedy lies in arranging circumstances so that distraction requires effort rather than abstention: a closed door, a silenced device, a task defined narrowly enough that its end is visible.

Rhythm matters as much as architecture. Most individuals possess a few hours each day in which their faculties are demonstrably sharper, and those hours are routinely surrendered to correspondence that could wait. Guarding that window for the most demanding work, and relegating the shallow chores to the weary remainder of the day, costs nothing and often doubles what is accomplished.

Nor should solitude be mistaken for idleness. Some of the most valuable cognitive labour is invisible: a mind turning over a difficulty, testing a connection, discarding it and trying another. Organisations that read quiet as inertia will, sooner or later, punish the very behaviour that generates their best ideas, and reward instead a frantic responsiveness that produces little of lasting worth.

The prescription, then, is neither austere nor mystical. Treat attention as a finite budget, defend it with structure rather than sentiment, and accept that depth is cultivated by what one declines as much as by what one undertakes. Those who learn this discipline find that their finest work was never prevented by a shortage of talent, only by a shortage of uninterrupted time.

## Syntax Dissection

- **Subject**: Modern organisations
- **Verb**: pay (coordinated with "furnish")
- **Object**: handsomely for people (first clause); those same people (second clause)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Relative Clause Modifying "people"]: who can reason through tangled problems | Defines the kind of worker the organisations value.
  - [Past Participial Phrase Modifying "workplaces"]: engineered to fragment every hour into scraps | Shows the deliberate design behind the disruption.
  - [Absolute Construction]: each one claimed by a message, a meeting, or a banner sliding across the screen | Itemises the intruders with a tricolon.
- **Analysis**: Two coordinated main clauses joined by "yet" set the reward for deep thinking against the habits that destroy it. The closing absolute phrase and its three-part list add concrete detail without a new finite verb.

### Sentence 2
- **Sentence**: Yet resolve is a perishable resource, and a strategy that demands its constant renewal is destined to fail by mid-afternoon.
- **Subject**: resolve (first clause); a strategy that demands its constant renewal (second clause)
- **Verb**: is (first clause); is destined (second clause)
- **Object**: a perishable resource (complement of the first clause)
- **Subordinate Clauses**:
  - [Relative Clause Modifying "strategy"]: that demands its constant renewal | Explains why the strategy cannot last.
- **Analysis**: A compound sentence opened by the contrastive "Yet". The first clause states a general truth and the second draws the practical consequence, with the infinitive "to fail" completing the passive-sounding "is destined".
